The Calorie Myth

Every nutrition label in the United States lists (and leads with!) Calories. Where did that number come from? Work backward through four exhibits from the present to 1819 to find out. Solve each to open the next.

  1. Exhibit AToday
  2. Exhibit B1896

Exhibit A | Today

The Label

Here’s a sample label for a granola bar. The Calories on a label can be calculated from its grams of protein, carbohydrate, and fat, using three numbers called Atwater factors: 4 Calories per gram of protein, 4 per gram of carbohydrate, and 9 per gram of fat.

Use the Atwater factors to calculate the bar’s Calories.

Hint

(protein × 4) + (carbs × 4) + (fat × 9)

Solved

Exhibit B | 1896

The Box

So, where did the 4, 9, and 4 come from? In 1896, Wilbur Olin Atwater, a chemist at Wesleyan University in Connecticut, began a series of nearly 500 experiments with his team. Each relied on a sealed chamber in the university’s Judd Hall.

A volunteer lived inside for days at a time while the team measured the oxygen going in, carbon dioxide coming out, and heat the volunteer’s body gave off.

Nearly 500 experiments over about ten years: about how many experiments a year is that?

Talk it overWhy put a whole person in a box instead of just burning the food and measuring the heat?

Solved

“Historical Row: Counting Calories.” Wesleyan University Magazine, 20 Sept. 2004, magazine.blogs.wesleyan.edu/2004/09/20/historical-row-counting-calories.

McSweegan, Edward. “Calorie Counting Gets Its Start in Connecticut.” New England Historical Society, newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/calorie-counting-gets-its-start-in-connecticut.

Exhibit C | 1887

The Magazine

Before he stuck some poor sap in a box, Atwater had to explain his unit of choice to the public. In 1887, he introduced the Calorie to American readers in The Century Magazine.

He defined it as the heat that would raise a kilogram of water one degree, and he also put it in terms of work: one Calorie is about 1.53 foot-tons, the work of lifting a ton about a foot and a half.

How many foot-tons is the granola bar’s 210 Calories? Round your answer to the nearest whole number.

Hint

calories × 1.53

Talk it overThose foot-tons assume perfect efficiency. Is a human body perfectly efficient?

Solved

Hargrove, James L. “Does the History of Food Energy Units Suggest a Solution to ‘Calorie Confusion’?” Nutrition Journal, vol. 6, 2007, article 44, https://doi.org/10.1186/1475-2891-6-44.

“How Counting Calories Became a Science.” IEEE Spectrum, spectrum.ieee.org/how-counting-calories-became-a-science.

Exhibit D | 1819

The Steam Engine

Atwater didn’t invent the Calorie. In 1819, Nicolas Clément was teaching industrial chemistry at the Conservatoire des Arts et Métiers in Paris. He needed a unit of heat to explain how steam engines turn heat into work.

His Calorie measured the heat needed to warm a kilogram of water from 0 to 1 °C. Today, scientists measure energy in joules instead. One Calorie equals 4.184 kilojoules.

Convert the bar’s 210 Calories to kilojoules. Round to the nearest whole number.

Hint

calories × 4.184

Solved

The Verdict

The calorie was never designed to count your lunch; it was designed to measure a steam engine.
